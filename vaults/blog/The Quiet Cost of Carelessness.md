*On AI, mindfulness, and what we lose when we stop being intentional with our tools*

AI is a force multiplier. That's both cool and scary, because what it multiplies depends on what you bring to it. Curious, critical thinkers get a boost. Lazy or careless users get deskilled. The tool isn't doing either of those things to people, it's just amplifying what's already there.

This isn't new. Every tool we've ever invented has worked this way. The printing press multiplied both literacy and propaganda. The calculator multiplied both mathematical capacity and the atrophy of mental arithmetic. Every leap in productivity has come with a corresponding leap in deskilling somewhere. That's just how progress works.

So AI isn't interesting because it's "the most effective tool yet." Tools have always kept getting more effective. What's interesting is that AI is unusually general AND unusually effective at the same time. Most tools are one or the other. A scalpel is effective but specialised. A pocket knife is general but limited. AI is both, competent enough at enough things to be the default tool you reach for, regardless of the task (up to a point).

Even that isn't quite unprecedented though. We've seen this before, recently.

The smartphone.

I recently watched a video from someone who did a month without their smartphone. They redesigned their life around it: books in their bag, an iPod for music, a wristwatch, a paper journal. Two things really stood out to me from their experience.

First, they didn't actually have less goofing-off time. About the same, really. What changed was their mindfulness about picking up tools. When one device handles 20 different functions, you stop thinking about which function you actually want. You check the time, see a notification, end up on social media, lose six hours. Without their phone, the desires themselves ("I want to listen to music," "I want to read this book," "I want to reach out to a friend") required more thought to act on. Pick up the iPod, the book, the (dumb)phone, instead of the same reflexive gesture toward the same device. Same activities, more intentional, because the tool wasn't a single attention-sink dressed as 20 different things.

Second, and this one is more counter-intuitive: the older tools took a bit more effort and a bit more time, and that effort actually made the experience better, not worse. There's a well-documented psychological pattern here, sometimes called the IKEA effect, or effort justification, where we derive more satisfaction from things we've put effort into. Convenience optimises for time saved, but not always for satisfaction. Sometimes the friction is the point.

AI is going to do all of this at another order of magnitude. One tool for writing, research, planning, coding, designing, talking through problems, making decisions. The convenience is real. And so is the risk.

But the stakes are different from the smartphone's. With the phone, it costs you your attention. With AI, it costs you your reasoning.

When AI handles the research, the planning, the design, the writing, the debugging, and you let it own every step along the way, the process becomes a black box. You see the output without seeing the work, and the work is where understanding lives. Problem-solving isn't just a means to an end, it's how you build the muscles to solve the next problem, and the one after that. Delegate it consistently and those muscles atrophy. You don't just lose sight of what you're doing, you lose the ability to think it through at all.

We might also lose the satisfaction that came from doing things the slower way, without really realising what we've traded for the speed.

That trade has costs we're only starting to understand. We've already seen what happens when convenience-optimised tools meet human psychology at scale: the smartphone and social media era has tracked alongside rising rates of anxiety and depression, especially in younger users. The causation is contested, but the pattern is serious enough that real research is warranted. AI is a more powerful version of the same pattern. It removes friction from things that used to require effort, including the effortful kinds of thinking and creating that give people a sense of meaning and agency. If the satisfaction from doing things ourselves is part of what keeps us psychologically well, outsourcing more of that to a tool is something to approach with caution, not just enthusiasm.

If there are psychologists or researchers in my network thinking about effort, agency, and well-being, I'd love to see (or help design) studies to test what happens when AI removes effort from work that used to provide meaning.

So here's the invitation: be conscious about it.

Notice when you reach for AI. Ask yourself what it is you actually want from this moment, and whether AI is the right tool for it. Sometimes it absolutely is, and using it will make you sharper, faster, more capable. Sometimes the better answer is the slower one. Think it through yourself, write the first draft alone, sit with the problem before asking for help. Sometimes the satisfaction you're looking for is on the other side of the effort that the tool would remove.

This applies to every tool, not just AI. Your phone, your favourite app, the autocomplete in your editor, the search engine you use without thinking. The question regularly worth asking is: what am I actually doing right now, and is this tool helping me do it, or doing it for me, or doing something else entirely that I didn't actually want?

Progress has been accelerating for a while now. AI accelerates it further. The tools demand less and less mindfulness from us. Which makes intentional mindfulness more valuable than ever.

How you use your tools matters more than whether you use them.

---

A note on terms: when I say “AI” above, I mostly mean LLMs in chatbot form and the coding tools built on them: Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Codex, etc. Other forms of AI, like image and music generation, share some of these concerns, especially the loss-of-effort one, but usually not the loss-of-reasoning one.

A note in the spirit of transparency: I used AI in the process of writing this, mostly as a glorified thesaurus, a sounding board, and a proof-reader. I did not use it to come up with the ideas, understand existing concepts, or write the drafts.
